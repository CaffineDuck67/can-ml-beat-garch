# Can Machine Learning Beat GARCH? A Walk-Forward Benchmark for S&P 500 Volatility Forecasting Using Macro Data

**Working paper — reproducible code accompanying this document produces every
number quoted below. Research only; not investment advice.**

---

## Abstract

We benchmark four classical volatility forecasters (a historical-mean
benchmark, exponentially weighted moving average, GARCH(1,1) with Student-t
innovations, and the HAR-RV regression) against four machine-learning models
(Ridge, Random Forest, LightGBM gradient boosting, and a small LSTM) for
predicting the future *realized volatility* of the S&P 500 at weekly (5-day) and
monthly (21-day) horizons. Predictions are generated by a strictly
expanding-window **walk-forward** procedure with a horizon-length **purge /
embargo**, so that every forecast is genuinely out-of-sample and no forward-looking
target overlaps training data. Features combine lagged realized volatility,
return moments, the VIX, the variance-risk premium, the yield curve, financial
conditions, oil, and publication-lag-corrected macro series. We evaluate with
RMSE, MAE, the robust QLIKE loss, out-of-sample *R*², and **Diebold–Mariano
(DM)** tests with Newey-West HAC variance and the Harvey small-sample
correction. We add an economic-value test (volatility targeting with
transaction costs), SHAP-based interpretability, and a **leakage demonstration**.

The honest conclusion is *mixed*: at the **5-day horizon machine learning wins
significantly** (Ridge RMSE 7.57 vs GARCH 8.56, DM *p* = 0.0002), but at the
**21-day horizon it does not beat the classical models** — no ML model
outperforms GARCH or HAR-RV significantly, and LightGBM is *significantly worse*
than HAR-RV (DM *p* = 0.016). Volatility targeting driven by the GARCH forecast
delivers the best risk-adjusted performance, so ML adds no clear economic value.
Methodologically, a naive random-split cross-validation inflates apparent
accuracy by **31% (5-day) to 63% (21-day)** relative to walk-forward,
underscoring why leakage control is the central contribution.

## 1. Introduction

Volatility is the fundamental input to option pricing, portfolio construction,
and risk management. The classical workhorses — GARCH (Bollerslev, 1986;
Student-t innovations) and the Heterogeneous AutoRegressive model of realized
volatility, HAR-RV (Corsi, 2009) — are linear-ish, parsimonious, and famously
hard to beat out of sample (Hansen & Lunde, 2005). A growing literature applies
machine learning — kernels, trees, and recurrent networks — to the same problem,
sometimes with large reported gains. Many such gains, however, are artifacts of
**evaluation leakage**: random cross-validation folds on serially correlated,
overlapping targets let a model effectively see its own forecast window.

This project asks a narrow, falsifiable question: **when evaluated honestly, can
ML beat GARCH (and HAR-RV) for forecasting S&P 500 realized volatility using
macro and market data?** We deliberately design the study to punish shortcuts:

1. pure expanding-window **walk-forward** forecasting, never random splits;
2. **purge/embargo** of *h* days between training end and test start to remove
   overlapping-target leakage;
3. **publication-lag** shifts on monthly macro so only genuinely public data
   enters features at date *t*;
4. unit tests that prove features at *t* are invariant to perturbations of data
   after *t*;
5. formal **DM** significance testing rather than eyeballing RMSE;
6. an explicit **leakage demonstration** quantifying the error of the wrong
   methodology.

We report what we find, including where ML *loses*.

## 2. Data

### 2.1 Prices and volatility inputs
S&P 500 index (`^GSPC`) and VIX (`^VIX`) daily closes are downloaded via
`yfinance` from 2000-01-01. Log returns are computed from the index.

### 2.2 Macro (FRED, public endpoint)
We retrieve, without an API key, the public `fredgraph.csv` series: DGS10, DGS2,
T10Y2Y (yield-curve slope), DFF (fed funds), UNRATE, CPIAUCSL, DCOILWTICO (WTI),
BAMLH0A0HYM2 (high-yield OAS), and NFCI. Requests use retries with exponential
backoff and time out to a `pandas-datareader` fallback; a `fredapi` path is used
if `FRED_API_KEY` is present. Downloads are cached and logged (row counts, date
ranges, missingness) to `data/raw/download_log.json`.

### 2.3 A documented substitution
The ICE BofA US High-Yield OAS series (`BAMLH0A0HYM2`) was **retired from FRED's
public history in 2024** and now serves only ~2023 onward — 16.5% coverage of our
feature window. Rather than discard 2015–2023 (or silently forward-fill a
vacuum), we substitute the **Chicago Fed National Financial Conditions Index
(NFCI)**, a weekly index with full history since 1971, for the credit/financial
stress features (`stress_level`, `stress_chg`). The decision is recorded in
`data/processed/feature_notes.json`. This is a limitation (Section 7): the stress
features change economic meaning and NFCI is broader than a pure credit spread.

### 2.4 Alignment and leakage control
All series are aligned to the S&P 500 trading calendar. Daily series are
forward-filled only. Monthly series (UNRATE, CPIAUCSL) are **shifted forward by
their publication lag before** forward-filling, so an observation for reference
month *m* can first be used on/after *m* + lag. Rows with missing features are
dropped only *after* feature construction.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Target
The forecast target is an annualized forward realized-volatility proxy, expressed
in percent (VIX-comparable):

> RV_h(t) = sqrt( (252 / h) * Σ_{i=1..h} r_{t+i}² ) · 100,  with h ∈ {5, 21}.

This uses returns from *t+1* to *t+h*; it is the *label*, never a feature.

### 3.2 Features (information up to and including *t*)
21 predictors: realized-vol components RV{1,5,21,63} (HAR building blocks);
lagged returns r{1,5,21}; 63-day return skew, kurtosis, and downside
semi-variance; VIX level, VIX 5-day change, and the variance-risk premium
(VIX − RV21); yield-curve slope, 21-day change in the 10Y yield and in the fed
funds rate; NFCI stress level and 21-day change; 21-day oil return; 3-month
unemployment change; CPI year-over-year. Calendar dummies were evaluated and
omitted as noise. A dedicated test perturbs all post-*t* observations and asserts
that features at *t* are unchanged (`tests/test_no_leakage.py`).

### 3.3 Models
*Classical:* historical-mean (expanding), EWMA (λ = 0.94), **GARCH(1,1)-t**
fitted with the `arch` package on an expanding window and propagated to horizon
*h* by continuing the variance recursion with **fixed** parameters, and **HAR-RV**
OLS on daily/weekly/monthly RV components.

*Machine learning:* Ridge (α tuned by `TimeSeriesSplit` CV **inside the training
window only**), Random Forest, LightGBM, and a small single-layer LSTM. To keep
the walk-forward affordable, the grid search is run **once on the oldest training
window** and the winning hyperparameters are reused for subsequent re-fits, which
re-train on the expanding sample. Because tuning only ever uses the strictly
historical first window, no test or future information enters selection. Random
seeds come from `config.yaml`.

### 3.4 Walk-forward validation
An ~8-year initial training window; forecasts are produced for every trading day
from `oos_start` onward (2010-01-01 in the full configuration; 2015-01-01 in the
fast run reported here). Models re-fit on a cadence (ML/HAR every 21 trading
days; GARCH/LSTM less often for runtime). For horizon *h*, the final *h* training
rows are purged so no training target window overlaps the test block. **No random
splits are used for any headline result.**

### 3.5 Evaluation
Per model and horizon, purely OOS: RMSE, MAE, **QLIKE** = ln(f²) − ln(a²) + a²/f²,
and out-of-sample *R*² vs the historical-mean benchmark. **Diebold–Mariano**
tests compare each model to GARCH and HAR-RV on squared-error loss differentials,
with a Newey-West HAC variance using (h−1) lags to absorb overlap
autocorrelation and the Harvey–Leybourne–Rounsiville (1997) small-sample
correction. Positive DM ⇒ the candidate has lower loss than the benchmark.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Forecast accuracy

**Table 1 — OOS accuracy (RMSE %, MAE %, QLIKE, R²_oos).** OOS window
2015-01-02 → 2026-10-09.

| Model | h=5 RMSE | h=5 MAE | h=5 QLIKE | h=5 R²_oos | h=21 RMSE | h=21 MAE | h=21 QLIKE | h=21 R²_oos |
|-------|---------:|--------:|----------:|-----------:|----------:|---------:|-----------:|------------:|
| hist_mean | 11.078 | 7.349 | 1.941 | 0.000 | 9.899 | 6.608 | 1.664 | 0.000 |
| EWMA | 9.314 | 5.850 | 1.598 | 0.293 | 9.512 | 5.435 | 1.620 | 0.077 |
| GARCH | 8.559 | 5.647 | 1.496 | 0.403 | 8.916 | 5.457 | 1.435 | 0.189 |
| HAR-RV | 8.150 | 5.123 | 1.547 | 0.459 | 8.304 | 4.979 | 1.467 | 0.296 |
| Ridge | **7.570** | 4.605 | 1.419 | **0.533** | 8.593 | 4.781 | 1.550 | 0.246 |
| RF | 7.677 | 4.670 | **1.405** | 0.520 | **8.080** | 4.755 | 1.463 | **0.334** |
| LightGBM | 7.744 | 4.794 | 1.432 | 0.511 | 9.185 | 5.745 | 1.532 | 0.139 |
| LSTM | 8.323 | 4.747 | 1.532 | 0.436 | 8.367 | 4.664 | 1.651 | 0.286 |

At **h = 5** the best classical model is HAR-RV (RMSE 8.150) yet every regularized
ML model beats it; Ridge is best overall (7.570). At **h = 21** the ranking
flips toward the classical side: RF (8.080) edges HAR-RV (8.304), but GARCH,
Ridge and LightGBM are all ≥ 8.6, and LightGBM (9.185) is worse than EWMA.

### 4.2 Statistical significance (Diebold–Mariano)

**Table 2 — DM statistics (p-value); positive ⇒ model beats benchmark.**

| Model | h=5 vs GARCH | h=5 vs HAR | h=21 vs GARCH | h=21 vs HAR |
|-------|:------------:|:----------:|:-------------:|:-----------:|
| Ridge | +3.76 (0.0002) | +3.36 (0.0008) | +0.45 (0.656) | −0.58 (0.562) |
| RF | +2.75 (0.0060) | +2.06 (0.039) | +1.39 (0.165) | +0.71 (0.476) |
| LightGBM | +2.45 (0.014) | +1.60 (0.109) | −0.49 (0.626) | **−2.41 (0.016)** |
| LSTM | +0.46 (0.643) | −0.38 (0.702) | +0.88 (0.377) | −0.18 (0.859) |

The weekly-horizon gains are **significant**: Ridge, RF and LightGBM all beat
GARCH at the 5% level, and Ridge/RF beat HAR-RV. The monthly-horizon gains are
**not significant** against either benchmark, and LightGBM is significantly
* inferior* to HAR-RV. This is the paper's central, honest finding: **ML's edge is
horizon-dependent and disappears (or reverses) at longer horizons.**

## 5. Robustness and interpretability

### 5.1 Sub-period analysis
Restricting to crisis windows does not overturn the story but sharpens it.
At **h = 21** during COVID-2020, RMSEs soar (HAR 21.17 best; EWMA is *worse than
the historical mean*, R²_oos = −0.059). During the **2022 rate shock**, Ridge is
best (5.29) while **LightGBM collapses** (RMSE 9.98, R²_oos = −0.26) — a clear
example of a tree ensemble extrapolating poorly on a regime it rarely saw in
training. Under **high stress (VIX > 30)** at h = 21 the classical HAR (18.62)
and RF (18.37) lead. At **h = 5** in calm markets Ridge/RF lead (≈ 5.5), and in
high-VIX regimes Ridge (20.36) edges HAR (21.21).

### 5.2 SHAP: what drives the tree model
Global mean(|SHAP|) for the LightGBM model is dominated by **VIX level (4.08)**,
then **financial-stress change (1.01)**, **21-day return (0.80)**, **downside
semi-variance (0.70)**, and **5-day return (0.52)**; macro terms (CPI YoY,
unemployment change, yield changes) contribute materially less. VIX and stress
carry most weight in both calm and high-VIX periods, confirming that most of
ML's edge is a flexible use of the option-implied volatility term structure
rather than the macro block.

### 5.3 Leakage demonstration
Re-running the *same* LightGBM model under naive random 5-fold CV versus honest
walk-forward:

| Horizon | Random K-fold RMSE | Walk-forward RMSE | Inflation |
|---------|-------------------:|------------------:|----------:|
| h = 5 | 5.339 | 7.744 | **31.1%** |
| h = 21 | 3.414 | 9.185 | **62.8%** |

Random splitting makes the model look 1.5× to 2.7× more accurate than it truly is
out of sample. This is precisely the trap the design is built to avoid.

### 5.4 Economic value (volatility targeting)
Position = clip(target_vol / forecast_vol, 0, 1.5×), rebalanced every 21 days,
5 bps per unit turnover.

| Strategy | Ann. return | Ann. vol | Sharpe | Max DD | Turnover |
|----------|------------:|---------:|-------:|-------:|---------:|
| Buy & hold | 11.60% | 17.66% | 0.657 | −36.1% | 0.00 |
| Vol-target GARCH | 10.20% | **14.97%** | **0.682** | **−23.0%** | 0.124 |
| Vol-target RF (ML) | 10.52% | 15.77% | 0.667 | −28.4% | 0.091 |

Both vol-targeting portfolios cut drawdowns sharply versus buy-and-hold, but the
**GARCH-forecast** version attains the highest Sharpe and the shallowest
drawdown; the ML (RF) forecast does not add economic value here.

### 5.5 Bonus regime classifier
For the "next-21-day max drawdown > 5%" flag (base rate 0.222), logistic
regression reaches PR-AUC 0.347 / Brier 0.178 while LightGBM is essentially at
base rate (PR-AUC 0.225 / Brier 0.206). Skill is weak and we report it as such.

## 6. Conclusion

Evaluated with strict, leakage-controlled walk-forward forecasting, **machine
learning beats GARCH and HAR-RV only at the weekly horizon, and not at the
monthly horizon**, where boosted trees can even underperform the classical
forecasts significantly. Most of the ML advantage traces to a flexible use of
the VIX and financial-conditions inputs rather than to the macro block. Volatility
targeting with GARCH forecasts delivers the best risk-adjusted outcome, so we
find **no robust evidence that ML adds economic value over classical
volatility modelling** for this asset and sample. The most transferable result is
methodological: **random-split evaluation inflates apparent accuracy by
31–63%**, a reminder that the benchmark, not the model class, is often the real
story.

---

## 7. Limitations

- **Fast-mode scope.** The numbers here come from the `--fast` configuration
  (OOS 2015-01-02 onward, coarser GARCH/LSTM re-fit cadence) for reproducibility
  within minutes. The full configuration extends the OOS window to 2010-01-01 and
  re-fits more frequently; point estimates will shift though the qualitative
  ML-wins-at-5-loses-at-21 conclusion is expected to persist.
- **Realized-vol proxy.** We use a rolling-std of daily returns rather than
  intraday/high-frequency RV, which is a noisier, microstructure-biased target.
- **Credit-proxy substitution.** NFCI stands in for the FRED-retired HY OAS,
  altering the interpretation of the stress features.
- **Single asset.** One index; conclusions need not generalize.
- **Under-tuned LSTM / once-tuned hyperparameters.** Runtime constraints cap model
  capacity and fix tuned parameters after the first window.
- **Backtest simplicity.** Costs are a flat 5 bps with monthly rebalancing; no
  slippage, funding, or margin costs.
